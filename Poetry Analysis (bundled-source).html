A Close Reading · Long Poem Format

Spring and Fall

a poem by Gerard Manley Hopkins  ·  annotated by the reader

Hopkins wrote this fourteen-line lyric in 1880 and addressed it to a child named Margaret, watching her grieve the falling leaves of an autumn wood. What looks at first like a poem about seasons turns, by its end, into something far stranger and more devastating — a poem about why we cry at all, and what we are really crying for.

Read it slowly. The sound is doing as much work as the sense.

§ I — The opening address
Address

The poem opens with a name — an act of attention. Márgarét, áre you gríeving — three accent marks in five words, like a finger laid gently under the chin to lift a child's face. Hopkins doesn't begin with the leaves, or the season. He begins with the person doing the looking.

The accent marks are scansion: he wants you to hear which syllables to lean on. Read it aloud and the line wobbles forward like someone walking on tip‑toe.

Coinage

Goldengrove unleaving is two coined words pretending to be one place. Goldengrove sounds like somewhere on a map — you could almost find it — but it's nowhere; it's the name we give the world when its leaves turn yellow. Unleaving turns a noun into a process: the grove is doing its leaving, and also un‑doing its leaves.

This is Hopkins's whole technique in a phrase. He invents words because the existing ones aren't moving fast enough.

Question

The question he asks her is delicate to the point of being impossible: do you, with your fresh thoughts — thoughts so new they haven't been crusted over by experience — care for falling leaves the way you'd care for a person?

The half‑rhyme between man, you and can you? is doing something almost embarrassing: it's making the syntax stumble, as if the speaker is trying not to lead the witness.

Pivot

And then, with the soft sigh of Áh!, the poem pivots. We leave Margaret's small grief and step into a much colder room: the room of the adult heart, which has seen this before and will see it again, and which has made its peace by going numb.

Notice the rhyme — older / colder. Hopkins isn't being subtle. The two words mean almost the same thing here.

Compound

And here come two more inventions: wanwood (pale, pining wood) and leafmeal (modeled on piecemeal — leaves, broken into a meal of themselves on the forest floor). Stack them together and you have a phrase that means, roughly, even if the entire world rotted into mulch.

Reversal

And here, halfway through line nine, the poem performs its great reversal. And yet you will weep and know why.

That italicized will — Hopkins didn't actually italicize it on the page, but the meter does — is the hinge. Will is both prophecy and verb of intention: you will weep, and one day you'll know why; or, you have it in you to weep, and to know.

Sound

Sórrow's spríngs áre the same — three stresses, all on long open vowels, like three slow bells. And listen to that s alliteration weaving through the line: sorrow's springs, the same. The sound itself springs.

Springs here means sources — the wells from which sorrow rises. But the word also rhymes us back to the title, the season we never reach in this poem.

Apophatic

These two lines describe a thing by listing what it is not. The mouth has not said it. The mind has not formulated it. The heart has only heard of it, vaguely; the ghost — that is, the soul, the geist — has only guessed.

Three internal alliterations — mouth/mind, heart/heard, ghost/guessed — each a tiny shrinking down: from speech, to thought, to feeling, to intuition.

Doctrine

And what does the dark contain? The blight man was born for.

This is the poem's plainest theological line. Blight is what happens to leaves; man was born for it — born into it, born toward it. Original sin in five syllables, but more than that: mortality, the simple fact that everything one loves will fall.

The leaves were never about the leaves.

Final Turn

And then the last line, which is one of the loveliest and cruelest endings in English poetry: It is Márgarét you mourn for.

The child weeping for the leaves is, without knowing it, weeping for herself. Not the future Margaret who will die one day — or not only her — but this Margaret, here, now, in the autumn wood, already in the process of becoming someone who will look at falling leaves without grief.

Hopkins gives her name three accents, like a small bell rung three times. Read aloud, the line takes almost too long to say.

Interlude · The Poet

A Jesuit who burned his poems before he wrote them.

Gerard Manley Hopkins was born in 1844 in Stratford, then a leafy suburb east of London. He was the eldest of nine. His father wrote insurance contracts and amateur poetry; his mother read German and music. The household was Anglican, devout, and bookish.

At Oxford he was Walter Pater's student and Robert Bridges's friend. In 1866, at twenty-two, he converted to Roman Catholicism — a decision so wrenching it estranged him from his family for years — and two years later entered the Society of Jesus as a novice. On the eve of his ordination he burned the poems he had written up to that point. He called it the slaughter of the innocents.

The world is charged with the grandeur of God. It will flame out, like shining from shook foil. — from "God's Grandeur," 1877

For seven years after the burning, he wrote almost nothing. Then in 1875 a German ship called the Deutschland went down in the Thames estuary, taking with it five Franciscan nuns exiled by Bismarck's anti‑Catholic laws. His superior remarked that someone should write a poem about it. Hopkins took the remark as permission. The dam broke.

He spent the rest of his short life as a priest — Liverpool, Glasgow, finally Dublin — teaching Greek to bored undergraduates and writing, in the margins of a punishingly unhappy life, some of the strangest and most musical poems in English. He published almost none of them. He died of typhoid in 1889, aged forty-four. His friend Bridges held the manuscripts back for thirty years before letting the world see them.

"Spring and Fall" was written in September 1880, in the Lake District, and is dated Lydiate, Lancashire. There is no historical Margaret. She is a composite, or an invention, or every child Hopkins had ever watched at the edge of an autumn lawn.

§ II — The music
Alliteration

Look at how the hard g of Goldengrove echoes itself, then ricochets one more time off the participle unleaving. Three g's, a triangle of consonants holding the line together.

Hopkins thought of poetry as a kind of music written for a single voice. He wrote in his notebooks about the way consonants chime against each other. This is what he meant.

Pun

The poem turns on a single word that gets quietly punned across all fourteen lines: leaves. They fall in line three, they're called unleaving in line two, they're leafmeal in line eight.

And of course to leave is also what people do. The dead leave us. The living, eventually, leave themselves.

Stress

Two clusters of stressed syllables come in a row: sights colder / By and by. Read it aloud and you can hear the meter slow almost to a stop. Hopkins is making the line itself feel colder — numbed, dragging.

This is sprung rhythm doing emotional work. The form is the meaning.

Coinage

Two w's huddled together in a single coined word: wanwood. They look as cold and exhausted as they sound. Wan — pale, drained — modifying wood, modifying nothing else, because Hopkins has just made up the compound.

He liked Anglo-Saxon kennings. Whale-road for the sea. Wanwood belongs in that lineage.

Repetition

The double m in mouth had, no nor mind hums like someone trying to find the right word and giving up. Mouth, mind — the two organs of expression, both negated.

This is one of the few places where Hopkins lets the consonants soften. After all the g's and w's, the m's feel almost tender, like humming to a child.

Last Word

And the last word that matters — mourn — rhymes with born in the line above. Born / mourn. The poem closes the gap between birth and grief, and the gap turns out to be a single vowel.

You enter the world. You leave it. In the middle there are leaves, falling, and a child watching them fall.

Interlude · The Form

Sprung rhythm, and why this poem doesn't sound like the others.

If you've read other Victorian poetry — Tennyson, Browning, Arnold — "Spring and Fall" should sound a little wrong. The lines aren't all the same length. The stresses are bunched up in places, spread thin in others. There are accent marks on the page, of all things. What is happening?

Hopkins called the meter he was writing in sprung rhythm, and he believed he had recovered it from the Old English alliterative tradition. The idea: count only the stressed syllables in each line. Let unstressed syllables fall where they may — one between each stress, or two, or none. The line gets its shape from its beats, not its syllable count.

This is, for one thing, how English actually sounds when spoken. We don't speak in iambs. We bunch our stresses; we trail off; we sometimes hit two hard syllables in a row. Sprung rhythm tries to put that on the page.

Sprung rhythm is the most natural of things... it is the rhythm of common speech and of written prose, when rhythm is perceived in them. — Hopkins, Author's Preface, 1883

It is also why Hopkins was unpublishable in his lifetime. His friend Robert Bridges — who became Poet Laureate, and who was the only person who really knew Hopkins's work — sat on the manuscripts until 1918, twenty-nine years after Hopkins's death. He wasn't being cruel. He thought the public wasn't ready.

And the leaves are still falling.
Margaret is still watching.
