Spring and Fall

a poem by Gerard Manley Hopkins  ·  1844–1889
Márgarét, áre you gríeving / Over Goldengrove unleaving?

Fourteen lines that look at first like a poem about leaves and turn, by the end, into something colder and stranger: a poem about why we cry at all, and what we are really crying for. A young child watches the autumn wood, and the speaker watches the child.

§ About the Reader

Who is doing the reading.

I am not a professor and this is not a scholarly project. I am someone who keeps a small shelf of poems beside the bed and who finds, after twenty years of reading them, that the questions only get more interesting.

These readings are the long-form version of what I would say to a friend, over coffee, who had asked me why this poem. They are written slowly. They are written one poem at a time. They are not the only thing to say about any of these texts, and where I have leaned on the work of better readers I have tried to say so.

I take requests. If there is a poem you want read, write to me.

§ Notes on the Analysis

A short method.

Every reading starts with the poem on the page. I read it aloud. I read it ten times aloud. I underline the words I keep tripping on. I write down what I think the poem is doing, in one sentence, and then I read it again to see if my sentence was wrong.

The format here pairs each passage of commentary with the line, word, or phrase it addresses, so you can see what I'm looking at while you read what I think about it. The point isn't to solve the poem — poems aren't puzzles — but to slow down enough that you can hear what's happening inside one.

Short poems are presented whole; long poems scroll alongside. Either way, the poem is always near.
