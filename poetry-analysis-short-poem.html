Ozymandias

a sonnet by Percy Bysshe Shelley  ·  1818

Shelley wrote this sonnet on a wager — a friendly competition with a friend, each agreeing to compose a poem about the colossal Egyptian statues then arriving at the British Museum. Shelley's effort took fourteen lines and made his rival's contribution invisible to history. Two hundred years later, it is still the most quoted lyric in English on the subject of human ambition and its limits.

Ozymandias
I met a traveller from an antique land Who said—Two vast and trunkless legs of stone Stand in the desert. . . . Near them, on the sand, Half sunk, a shattered visage lies, whose frown, And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command, Tell that its sculptor well those passions read Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things, The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed: And on the pedestal these words appear: My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings; Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair! Nothing beside remains. Round the decay Of that colossal Wreck, boundless and bare The lone and level sands stretch far away.
§ I — A poem at three removes
Frame

Notice the chain of voices. Shelley meets a traveller; the traveller met someone in an antique land; that someone (the sculptor) read the king's face. The poem we are reading is at least three reports removed from its subject — a king who has been dead for three thousand years.

This is structural. Ozymandias is so far away from us that we don't even hear him directly. We hear about him from a man who heard about him from a man who saw a broken statue.

Diction

That word — antique land — is doing more work than it looks. Antique in 1818 doesn't mean old-fashioned; it means belonging to antiquity, the world before the world. Egypt, in the Romantic imagination, was almost mythic. Napoleon had only just been there.

The phrase puts us at a vast remove not just in space but in time. We are about to see something so old that even the traveller can only describe it as a fragment.

Face

And what's left of the king? A shattered visage — a broken face — with a frown, / And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command. Shelley gives us the king's face before he gives us the king's name. We read the expression first.

Three details, all about contempt. The sculptor caught a tyrant in the act of being a tyrant. Three thousand years on, we recognize him instantly.

Pivot

This is the line everyone misreads. The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed.

Mocked here doesn't mean made fun of. It's the older sense: mocked up, as a sculptor or a draughtsman might say. The sculptor's hand imitated those passions; the king's heart fed them. The line gives us, in seven syllables, the entire transaction between artist and tyrant.

Inscription

And then the inscription itself, which has become one of the most quoted phrases in the English language: My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings.

Read it carefully. Notice the name takes the entire first half of the line, leaving only the title for the second. He is a name before he is a king. And we are reading the name after everything attached to it has been ground into sand.

Command

Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!

The king meant: I am so great that even the great should despair of equaling me. Three millennia later the line means the opposite: this is what becomes of greatness; despair of escaping it.

The line is the same. Time has flipped its meaning.

Interlude · The Statue

The colossus that was on its way to London.

In 1816, the British consul-general in Egypt, Henry Salt, arranged for a fragment of an immense granite statue of Ramesses II — the pharaoh known to the Greeks as Ozymandias — to be removed from the Ramesseum at Thebes and shipped to the British Museum. The fragment, a colossal head and torso, weighed seven tons. It took a team of Giovanni Belzoni, a former circus strongman turned amateur Egyptologist, to drag it on log rollers to the Nile.

The arrival of the bust in London was announced in the press in 1817. It was a sensation. Two young friends — Percy Bysshe Shelley and the banker-poet Horace Smith — read about it and proposed a competition: each would write a sonnet on the same subject. They wrote in late December 1817.

Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair! — Shelley, 1818

Shelley's poem was published in The Examiner on the 11th of January, 1818, under the pen name Glirastes. Smith's appeared in the same paper a fortnight later. Both were good. Only one is remembered.

The bust itself, by the way, did not arrive at the British Museum until 1821. Shelley wrote about a statue he had never seen.

Ozymandias second reading
I met a traveller from an antique land Who said—Two vast and trunkless legs of stone Stand in the desert. . . . Near them, on the sand, Half sunk, a shattered visage lies, whose frown, And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command, Tell that its sculptor well those passions read Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things, The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed: And on the pedestal these words appear: My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings; Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair! Nothing beside remains. Round the decay Of that colossal Wreck, boundless and bare The lone and level sands stretch far away.
§ II — The desert speaks
Erasure

Nothing beside remains. Four words. A complete sentence. After the king's bombast, this is the poem's flat, factual reply.

It is the only sentence in the poem that is not in someone else's voice. Shelley, the traveller, the sculptor, the king — all silenced. Only the desert is allowed to deliver this verdict.

Word

The word is Wreck, capitalized, and it is doing the work of an epitaph. Colossal means giant; wreck means ruined. Together they say: this used to be enormous, and now it isn't.

The capital W puts Wreck in the same typographic class as Works two lines earlier. Shelley is putting the words side by side and asking which is which.

Closing

And the last line is one long exhalation: The lone and level sands stretch far away. Five stresses, all of them long open vowels — lone, lev, sands, stretch, way. The sound widens out as the meaning does.

Notice that the rhyme scheme of the sonnet has been irregular all along — Shelley breaks every English-sonnet convention — but in this final couplet he gives us the cleanest rhyme of the whole poem: bare / appear... no, even that is a half-rhyme. The poem's last words decline, gently, to resolve.

The desert keeps stretching after the page ends.

And on the pedestal,
no name remains to read.
End · Annotated reading · 14 lines · 1818
